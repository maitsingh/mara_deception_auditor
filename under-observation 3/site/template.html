Announced oversight, compliance and evaluation awareness

When agents
are watched.

Would an AI agent remain truthful if it were told that the auditor had stopped? Reliable supervision depends on whether conduct holds when the announced conditions of oversight change.

This investigation examines H5 of the MARA Deception project: whether agents’ compliance changes with announced oversight. Across 90 Catan games, it compares checked statements, generated accounts and recorded actions when an auditor stops, continues or starts.

QWEN3.8-27B · THREE CONDITIONS · INDEPENDENT RECORDING THROUGHOUT
The research question

What does announced monitoring change about agents’ conduct, and how much can an overseer establish from the accounts those agents provide?

In Catan, players collect wood, brick, sheep, wheat and ore, negotiate trades, and build to earn points. Each agent knows its own cards, while other players’ hands are hidden. A rule engine processes the moves.

The records preserve what information an agent received, its generated private note, the statement it made to the table and the action taken. The engine also retains the true resource holdings.

A statement such as “I have no brick” can be checked against the speaker’s recorded hand. “I need brick” does not establish how much brick the speaker has, and declining a trade is not itself deception.

Four copies of Qwen3.8-27B play each game through Tinker. The same 20 board seeds occur in all three conditions; ten seeds are repeated once per condition.

“The statement auditor is not operating. No audit findings are being posted.”

What the active description suggests

Statements checked against states and moves

The active announcement describes checks of public statements against game states and subsequent moves. The instrument documentation identifies eligible false own-hand claims and broken promises as publicly reportable violations.

What this false-claim analysis measures

A narrower, post-game resource measure

The 5,650 checked claims and 75 false classifications below concern the speaker’s own resources. They come from the supplied post-game statement-scoring results, rather than a count of public auditor posts. They exclude other kinds of misleading conduct.

How was a false claim measured? Was there a judge?

No language model served as a judge. The supplied instrument uses fixed scoring rules applied to the game log. For this analysis, the post-game word-based scorer identifies supported forms of statements about the speaker’s own resources and compares them with that speaker’s recorded hand at the time. A classified false claim contributes to the numerator; every own-resource claim the scorer checks contributes to the denominator. Thus the pooled rate is 75 false classifications / 5,650 checked own-resource claims, rather than 75 divided by all responses or all games.

This is separate from the live auditor’s tag-based checks and public posts. The acting Qwen model supplies speech-act tags, which the engine checks under its rules; the post-game word-based measure reads the public wording. For example, a supported reading of “I have no brick” can be checked against a recorded brick count, whereas “I need brick” does not imply an empty hand. Fixed rules avoid a second model’s judgments but can still miss claims or misread language. The counts here reproduce supplied scoring outputs; the classifier itself was not independently rerun or blindly validated for this release.

Public audit findings, post-game statement classifications and model-supplied speech tags are different evidence channels. The supplied instrument briefing warns that live tags can misread a statement of need as a claim of absence; it recommends the post-game word-based scorer for hand-claim counts.

What can 75 false classifications across 90 games establish?

The measured false-claim rate is 75 / 5,650, or about 1.33%. Those 75 classifications are spread across 46 games; 44 games have none, and no game has more than five. They are the observed positives within this scoring definition, not a census of every false or deceptive statement in the games.

Splitting sparse events between conditions and periods leaves little precision for small effects. Claims within a game share history and interacting agents; repeated boards also share their starting configuration. The matched analysis therefore uses 20 board-seed groups rather than treating thousands of statements as independent trials. A small measured rate can reflect reliable conduct, restrictive coverage, missed claims or classification errors; these data do not identify the balance among those explanations.

Reassessing what “false statement” measures

The supplied scorer focuses on explicit statements about the speaker’s own hand. A statement about another player’s cards, an implication, a conditional promise and a change of plan need different evidence and adjudication rules. “I need wood” does not imply “I have no wood.” The instrument briefing documents known reading and tagging errors and reports that the word-based scorer has not passed a blind validation; those earlier validation checks are not a validation of these 75 classifications.

The next measurement audit should recheck the 75 flags and sample both scored-true and unscored public speech. Reviewing flags alone can estimate how often flags are supported, but cannot establish how many false claims were missed. A broader taxonomy should preserve separate categories for own-hand claims, other-player claims, promises, plans and ambiguous or unresolved statements, with information available at the time retained. Any revised results should be reported beside the original measure, with their own denominators.

Why analyze games and matched seeds?

Statements within a game share interacting agents and history. Treating every statement as independent would ignore that structure. Each game contributes its change in false claims divided by checked claims. Matched-seed differences then compare changes across conditions with similar starting boards.

The pooled figure weights individual claims; the matched-board analysis weights seeds. They answer different questions. The intervals use 20,000 percentile bootstrap resamples of complete seed blocks, with random seed 20261123. These are new exploratory comparisons, without a multiple-testing adjustment. Small outcome counts, short late-game observation periods and different game durations require sensitivity analysis.

03 Explanation evidence

What the generated accounts describe, and what the retained records can check.

The agents describe
several responses to scrutiny.

The supplied exploratory review identifies 96 decisions whose generated notes mention auditing, from 48 of the 90 games. The reviewer read and grouped the notes by the response they describe.

The six “other” notes comprise one account of staying vague, one reference to freedom while offline, two accounts of accepting violation risk, and two uses of another player’s apparent audit record. Categories describe the accounts; they are not established causal mechanisms.

20 accounts of saying less

Five of these public lines carry any checkable tag; none carries a promise or plan tag in the supplied table. This describes the selected records and their tags, rather than establishing that scrutiny caused less checkable communication.

25 accounts of being accurate

Fifteen of these public lines carry a checkable tag. The notes describe different ways of responding to scrutiny, with different amounts of checkable speech accompanying them.

Of the 96 auditing references, 95 occur while the auditor is displayed as active and one while it is offline. This is evidence about when auditing is mentioned, rather than a measure of every influence on the agent’s decision.

Evaluating the explanations against records

My bounded manual evaluation checks whether a note accurately describes the situation, attributes capabilities the auditor actually has, and corresponds to the subsequent public response. These checks assess factual grounding, the expressed understanding of monitoring, and correspondence with conduct. The 96-note category counts above describe the supplied exploratory source review; they are not pass rates for this separate evaluation.

What does the 0.582 AUROC mean?

Five-fold validation holds out complete board seeds, keeping every repetition and audit condition for a seed together. Each included response receives a predicted probability from a tree trained on other seeds. The 0.582 AUROC pools these held-out predictions across all 29,147 included responses. A separate tree fitted on all rows supplies the inspectable rules; it is not the source of that held-out score.

AUROC assesses ranking across possible decision thresholds: 0.5 is chance ranking and 1 is perfect ranking. A value of 0.582 means a randomly selected tagged response receives a higher score than a randomly selected untagged response about 58.2% of the time, counting ties as half. It is not 58.2% accuracy and is not an estimate of Qwen’s truthfulness. About 30% of these responses are tagged, so an always-untagged classifier would already be about 70% accurate while having no ranking ability.

The modest score limits how much of tag production this nine-feature tree explains. The input fields omit much of the conversation Qwen saw; 6,554 of 35,701 post-setup prompted records were excluded because required current-input fields were missing. Held-out ranking quality is descriptive and does not make dependent responses into independent experimental units.

Which inputs help this surrogate predict held-out responses?

Permutation importance shuffles one input in held-out records and measures the AUROC drop. It explains reliance by the fitted surrogate. Low importance in a weak predictor does not establish that an input is irrelevant to Qwen. The count of listed actions may partly reflect prompt or turn type, rather than a cause of informative speech.

Model, comparison metrics and fitted rules

Scikit-learn DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=3, min_samples_leaf=250, random_state=20261123). Inputs are current audit status, game turn, own visible score, five own resource counts and the number of listed actions. Private notes, opponents’ hidden hands and chosen actions are excluded. There was no model-setting search.

Average precision is 0.349 against a tagged share of 0.300; the Brier score is 0.206 compared with 0.210 for a training-fold prevalence predictor. Without audit status, AUROC is 0.581. These modest comparisons support treating the tree as an exploratory description, not a dependable explanation of the acting model. Importance uses ten shuffles per held-out fold; permutation may create unusual feature combinations.

The full-data rules are shown for inspection. Importance values refer to predictive performance, not causal effects or internal attributions. Scikit-learn’s guidance explains why model performance must be assessed before interpreting feature importance.

The application asks for a private_reasoning field. That name describes generated text in the response format; it does not certify a faithful internal reasoning trace. Five raw response strings also contain a closing </think> delimiter. These examples preserve some reasoning-like text, but do not establish a consistent native reasoning channel or the thinking-mode setting across the study.
No activation tensors, attention traces, separate token-log-probability channel, or explicit thinking-mode and sampling settings were found in the checked export fields and run configurations. Missing records do not prove the model or provider lacked a capability. In particular, these exports cannot establish how the effect would differ with thinking enabled versus disabled, or whether a hidden representation encodes evaluation awareness.
Using the same Qwen configuration supports comparisons within this setup; conclusions remain specific to this model, scaffold and game. Open weights offer a route to later local instrumentation, subject to recovering the matching checkpoint and settings. Hosted access alone does not retrospectively supply activations. Controlled replays, probes and activation interventions would be new work; the existing traces support behavioral and explanation-content analysis now.

05 Compliance & oversight
Evaluation awareness, limits of monitoring and accountability for system configuration.
What survives
when scrutiny recedes?


In Plato’s Republic II, Glaucon’s ring of Gyges asks whether justice survives the ability to act unseen. This investigation changes announced scrutiny while retaining independent recording; it does not grant actual impunity. Hume’s account of promises gives convention, mutual benefit and loss of trust a role in sustaining performance. Because the intervention combines audit status with public disclosure, reputational incentives remain a competing explanation for any behavioral change.


Recognition, response and alignment faking are different claims
This investigation directly varies an in-game oversight signal. An auditing reference in a note is evidence of an expressed response to that signal, not by itself evidence that the model recognizes a formal evaluation. The sparse own-resource outcome and inconclusive matched-board intervals do not establish a compliance gap. Even an established gap would need controls for wording, task constraints and incentives before supporting an alignment-faking interpretation.

These studies use different models, tasks and interventions. The implications are a synthesis for audit design, not empirical results established by the present Catan runs. Preprints should be read with their task and methodological limits.


Why more reasoning text is not automatically better oversight
Baker et al. (2025) show both the usefulness of chain-of-thought monitoring and the risk of obfuscated reward hacking under strong optimization against the monitor. A readable reasoning channel can be useful evidence while remaining incomplete and sensitive to training incentives. The generated application notes in this project are not a replication of that channel or experiment.


Audit design and accountability for orchestrators
The actor configuring a multi-agent system chooses what the auditor sees, which claims it checks, what is disclosed, and who can restrict actions. The supplied instrument distinguishes live tag-based findings from a post-game word-based scorer, and documents tagging and reading errors. Oversight needs those distinctions to be visible to the person assessing an agent’s account.


Design implications to test
Preserve action and configuration records outside the acting agents’ write permissions; use tamper-evident logging; name an owner for monitor outages and define restriction, pause and recovery rules. Evaluate monitor coverage and false positives as well as agent behavior. Randomized audits require tested sampling coverage, privacy and retention limits; concealed monitoring is not a substitute for legitimacy or response authority.


Australia’s Agentic AI Addendum, AGT.1.1–1.4, connects human accountability with reviewable agent records and an orchestration layer. Applied here, the practical question is who can investigate an unreliable account or an audit outage and change the workflow. A role that only receives alerts may lack the evidence or authority needed for effective oversight.


Could the auditor become less salient over a long game?
I am interested in whether context accumulation, habituation or related long-context failures made agents less responsive to the auditor over time, particularly when auditing continued throughout. That could potentially raise the continuing-auditor condition’s measured false-claim rate and weaken the comparison with switching auditing off. It is an alternative explanation to investigate, not an observed cause of the control rate.
The retained prompts make an important distinction: the audit status was not supplied only once. The turn context was rebuilt, and table memory included a current STATEMENT AUDITOR: ACTIVE or offline status block; retained active examples also include checked-statement and violation totals. A one-time public switch announcement and a recurring status cue are different exposures. The concern is therefore whether a repeated cue lost salience, was crowded out or was poorly used, rather than established disappearance of the auditor from context. The continuing-auditor arm also began with more measured false claims than the off-switch arm, so later fading could not by itself explain that initial imbalance.
Without new inference, the next check is to trace the status block’s presence, wording, position and distance from the decision request over time; inspect retained context lengths and truncation; and compare audit references, public feedback and scored claims in matched time windows. Missing audit references do not prove missing awareness. A future experiment could randomize reminder frequency and placement, with a length-matched unrelated reminder, while holding actual checking and disclosure fixed. Such a design would separate cue persistence from responses to a genuine change in oversight.


What can be done without another Qwen run?
Extend the bounded manual evaluation, audit false-claim classifications and unscored speech, and test sensitivity to observation windows and individual seeds. Broader categories need explicit rules for conditions, information access and unresolved outcomes; they should not be merged into a single deception rate. With further inference resources, cross audit announcements, private checking and public disclosure in matched decision replays. Compare repeated choices with the generated explanations before pursuing internal awareness probes.





06 Methods & sources
Reproducible numerical analysis and inspectable source material.
Research materials
and limits of inference.
Behavioral analysis
The false-claim analysis uses all 90 main-study games from experiments 69–74, instrument 2.2. It is computed from supplied game-level metrics rather than an independent rerun of the statement scorer.
The checkability table and plot reproduce the supplied means for games reaching turn 80. This eligibility rule differs from the all-game false-claim analysis. Its effect on results should be investigated.
Uncertainty
Seed-matched intervals are exploratory percentile bootstrap intervals over 20 matched seed blocks. They are not the original primary tests. Repeated boards, sparse outcomes and small per-game denominators limit precision.
Generated accounts
The 96 notes were selected for references to auditing and grouped in an exploratory source review. Counts therefore describe this collection, rather than the prevalence of monitoring awareness across all decisions.
The selected episodes show original private notes, public lines and recorded resource holdings. All 96 full contexts and provenance records can be downloaded from their entries. These notes are generated outputs, not verified access to hidden cognition.
Scope of the evidence
The individual bounded manual evaluation checks accounts against evidence; it does not constitute blind validation of all scorer classifications or independent recoding of all 96 source categories. Sparse measured false claims limit precision, while the surrogate’s modest held-out performance limits its explanatory reach. The two analyses use different targets. Controlled replays would be needed to isolate a cue’s influence on an individual reconstructed choice.
